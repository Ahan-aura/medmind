Diagnostic errors in medicine are predominantly cognitive in origin, rooted in predictable heuristic traps rather than lack of factual knowledge. Anchoring bias occurs when a clinician locks onto an initial impression—such as assuming a tired young woman must have nutritional iron deficiency—and fails to adjust their diagnostic stance when subsequent laboratory findings (such as normal ferritin or profound basophilic stippling) conflict with that hypothesis.

Premature closure represents the failure to seek alternative explanations once an initial plausible diagnosis is identified, leading to missed dual pathology or underlying sinister causes. Employing cognitive forcing strategies, such as consciously asking 'What else could explain this laboratory pattern?' and 'What finding contradicts my leading hypothesis?', systematically improves diagnostic calibration and patient safety.
